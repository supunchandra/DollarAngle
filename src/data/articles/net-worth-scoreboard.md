A bigger salary can make you feel wealthier long before you actually become wealthier.

The upgraded apartment arrives first. Then the car. Then the subscriptions, restaurants, travel and financing plans that somehow turn the new salary into the old level of financial stress.

This is why income alone is a weak wealth scoreboard.

A more useful measure is **net worth**:

**assets minus liabilities.**

It is imperfect, but it forces an important question: **What do I actually own after subtracting what I owe?**

## Start with assets

Assets can include:

- checking and savings balances,
- retirement accounts,
- brokerage investments,
- home equity,
- business ownership,
- other property with meaningful resale value.

Be conservative with items such as cars, furniture and collectibles. Their resale values can be lower than owners imagine, and many depreciate over time.

## Then list liabilities

Liabilities can include:

- credit-card balances,
- student loans,
- personal loans,
- auto loans,
- mortgage balances,
- tax debt,
- other money you legally owe.

Subtract the total liabilities from the total assets.

The result can be positive or negative. Neither result is a moral judgment. It is a starting point.

## Why the trend matters more than one number

A young professional with student loans may have a negative net worth but be improving rapidly. A high-income household may have a positive net worth that barely changes because spending expands with every raise.

So track the direction.

A quarterly review is enough for many people. Daily net-worth tracking can create noise because investment prices move constantly.

Ask:

- Are debts shrinking?
- Are liquid savings growing?
- Are retirement assets growing because of contributions, not just market gains?
- Is home equity changing?
- Is lifestyle inflation consuming income growth?

## Do not confuse accounting assets with good investments

Robert Kiyosaki popularized a simple personal-finance framing: think about whether something puts money into your pocket or takes money out.

That can be a useful behavioral lens, but it is not formal accounting. A personal residence, for example, is an asset on a balance sheet even though it also generates ongoing cash outflows such as taxes, insurance and maintenance.

The practical lesson is to separate two questions:

1. Is this something I own with economic value?
2. Does owning it improve or weaken my cash flow?

Both matter.

## Net worth can rise while cash flow becomes fragile

Suppose most of your wealth is home equity and retirement accounts while you have almost no accessible cash.

Your net worth may look healthy, but a job loss can still create immediate stress.

That is why net worth should be paired with liquidity measures such as emergency savings and monthly cash-flow margin.

## Set a contribution goal, not a market-return goal

You cannot control what markets return next year.

You can control how much you save, how much debt you repay, how diversified you are and how much you pay in avoidable fees.

When reviewing net worth, separate:

- **contributions** you made,
- **debt reduction** you achieved,
- **market movement** you did not control.

This prevents a bull market from convincing you that overspending is a financial strategy.

## Watch for lifestyle leverage

Lifestyle inflation becomes especially dangerous when recurring upgrades are financed.

A higher salary can support a larger car loan, larger mortgage and larger credit limits. That can increase visible consumption while slowing net-worth growth.

The question is not whether you are allowed to enjoy higher income. You should.

The question is whether **some of every income increase becomes ownership**.

## The DollarAngle

Income is the engine. Net worth is the accumulated result.

Track the balance sheet often enough to see whether your choices are building ownership or merely financing a more expensive lifestyle.

The goal is not to maximize the number at the expense of living. It is to make sure your life today is not silently consuming your future.
