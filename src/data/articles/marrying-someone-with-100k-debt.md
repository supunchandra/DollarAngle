*You love them. You've imagined the wedding, the house and perhaps children. Then you discover they're carrying $100,000 in debt.*

## "You owe HOW much?"

Sophie and James were planning their wedding when a conversation about student loans became unexpectedly serious.

> **Sophie:** "How much do you still owe?"
>
> **James:** "Close to a hundred thousand, including the credit card."
>
> **Sophie:** "We are planning a future. Why didn't you tell me?"
>
> **James:** "I was embarrassed. I didn't want you to see me differently."

Sophie wasn't asking James to be wealthy. She wanted to know the financial situation they would face together.

*These characters and conversations are fictional.*

## 1. Not all $100,000 debts tell the same story

Educational loans, car loans and high-interest credit cards have different repayment costs. Two people may carry identical balances but have very different incomes, borrowing terms and financial habits.

| Example debt | Balance | Interest |
| --- | ---: | ---: |
| Education loans | $80,000 | 5.5% |
| Credit card | $15,000 | 24% |
| Car loan | $5,000 | 7% |

Ask what they owe, why they borrowed and what their repayment plan involves.

## 2. The bigger red flag may be secrecy

There is a difference between financial privacy early in dating and hiding important obligations before a joint mortgage or marriage.

> **James:** "I didn't actually lie."
>
> **Sophie:** "But you let me think we could afford a house next year without telling me your monthly payments."

The concern is not necessarily the debt itself. It's whether both partners can discuss difficult facts honestly and respectfully.

## 3. Could their debt become your responsibility?

Marriage does not automatically transfer every premarital debt to the other spouse in the United States. But liability may depend on state law, the type of debt and whether you sign a joint agreement.

Rules vary across the US, UK and Europe. Consult local legal guidance before cosigning, guaranteeing or combining borrowing. See the [Consumer Financial Protection Bureau](https://www.consumerfinance.gov/ask-cfpb/am-i-responsible-for-my-spouses-debts-after-they-die-en-1467/) for an introduction to US debt responsibility.

## 4. Love may be free, but cash flow isn't

Someone earning $75,000 annually has $6,250 gross monthly income. Suppose their monthly education, car and credit card payments total $1,400.

Their debt-to-income ratio is **22.4%**: $1,400 divided by $6,250. This does not measure taxes, food, rent or other basic expenses.

> **Sophie:** "So we can buy a house next year?"
>
> **James:** "I want us to."
>
> **Sophie:** "Wanting one and being ready are different things."

The [CFPB explains debt-to-income ratios](https://www.consumerfinance.gov/ask-cfpb/what-is-a-debt-to-income-ratio-en-1791/), which are useful but never the whole budget.

## 5. Should you help pay off your partner's debt?

Some couples maintain individual debt responsibility. Others agree to share repayment. Both approaches can be reasonable.

Before transferring savings, discuss emergency funds, ownership, whether the money is a gift or loan, and whether professional guidance would help.

> **James:** "You don't have to fix my debt."
>
> **Sophie:** "I know. I just want us to understand how it affects our future."

Financial teamwork should not eliminate individual security.

## 6. Can you build wealth while repaying large debts?

Start by listing all balances, rates and minimum payments. Make required payments, build suitable emergency savings and consider prioritizing expensive debt. Then evaluate retirement savings and long-term investing according to your circumstances.

Review the plan each month. Consistency matters more than pretending to solve everything overnight.

## 7. Five questions before saying "I do"

1. What do we each owe, and what are the loan terms?
2. Are there other financial commitments we haven't discussed?
3. How will we handle shared bills and personal money?
4. What if one of us loses income?
5. What concrete progress would make us both feel ready?

These questions can create clarity without turning love into an accounting exercise.

## You can love someone and still say "not yet"

A partner with significant debt and a realistic repayment plan may be a wonderful match. A couple may also choose to postpone major financial commitments while strengthening their finances.

What matters is honesty, a sustainable plan, personal boundaries and mutual respect.

## So, would Sophie marry James?

A week later, they sat at the kitchen table with a notebook of debts and monthly payments.

> **Sophie:** "I still want to marry you. But we need to be honest about the financial life we're choosing."
>
> **James:** "Then let's make a plan together."
>
> **Sophie:** "And maybe we don't need a wedding that costs another thirty thousand dollars."
>
> **James:** "I was hoping you'd say that."

They hadn't erased the debt. They had finally started talking about it.

**The real question isn't just 'How much debt do you have?' It's 'Can we face what it means together?'**
