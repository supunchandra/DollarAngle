Imagine you have three debts:

- a small medical balance,
- a medium personal loan,
- a large credit-card balance charging the highest interest rate.

You have enough cash flow to pay every minimum plus an extra amount each month. Where should the extra money go?

There are two classic answers: the **debt avalanche** and the **debt snowball**.

Both begin with the same rule: make at least the required payment on every debt. The difference is where you direct the extra money.

## The avalanche: highest interest rate first

With the avalanche method, list debts from highest interest rate to lowest.

You direct every available extra dollar to the highest-rate balance while making minimum payments on the others. When that debt is gone, roll its payment into the next-highest-rate debt.

Why it works: interest is a cost. Attacking the highest cost first usually reduces total interest expense when the payment amounts and timing are otherwise the same.

The weakness is psychological. The highest-rate debt may also be a large balance, which can mean months of effort before an account disappears completely.

## The snowball: smallest balance first

With the snowball method, ignore interest rates for the ordering and attack the smallest balance first.

When the first debt reaches zero, roll that payment into the next-smallest balance.

The advantage is visible progress. Closing an account can create momentum and reduce the number of bills competing for attention.

The disadvantage is financial: if a larger balance carries a much higher rate, leaving it for later can increase total interest paid.

The CFPB's debt action plan presents both methods and explicitly notes this tradeoff: smallest-balance payoff can create quick progress, while highest-rate payoff targets the most expensive debt first.

## Which method should you choose?

Start with the math, then be honest about behavior.

Choose the avalanche if:

- interest rates differ meaningfully,
- you are motivated by efficiency,
- you can stick with a plan even when progress feels slow,
- the high-rate debt is materially expensive.

Consider the snowball if:

- you have many small balances creating mental clutter,
- previous payoff plans failed because progress felt invisible,
- eliminating a small required payment would meaningfully improve monthly cash flow,
- the interest-rate differences are relatively small.

## A hybrid can be rational

Personal finance does not need to become a purity test.

You might eliminate one tiny balance for momentum and then switch to the avalanche. Or you might prioritize a debt with a variable rate, an expiring promotional rate or another risk that does not show up in a simple APR ranking.

The goal is not winning an argument about payoff methods. The goal is getting out of expensive debt and staying out.

## Fix the system that created the debt

A payoff plan can fail if the underlying cash-flow problem remains.

Ask why the balances accumulated:

- unexpected emergencies,
- recurring spending above income,
- medical costs,
- unemployment,
- irregular income,
- a one-time major purchase,
- lack of savings,
- habit or convenience spending.

The solution changes depending on the cause.

If emergencies repeatedly go onto cards, build a cash buffer while paying debt. If the issue is chronic overspending, reduce the recurring gap. If the debt followed a temporary income loss, rebuilding income stability may be the highest-return move available.

## Do not confuse debt payoff with investing return

Paying down a balance charging a high interest rate is not literally the same thing as earning that percentage in an investment account, because taxes, liquidity and other factors differ.

But the comparison is still useful: the cost of debt is contractual, while market returns are uncertain.

That is why carrying expensive revolving debt while aggressively speculating in stocks or crypto can create a strange risk profile—you are accepting a known high financing cost to pursue an uncertain return.

## What to do after the final payment

The most important month may be the one after the debt disappears.

Instead of letting the old payment vanish into lifestyle spending, redirect at least part of it automatically toward:

- emergency savings,
- retirement,
- a sinking fund,
- diversified investing,
- another priority debt.

That is how debt payoff turns into wealth building rather than merely resetting the cycle.

## The DollarAngle

The avalanche usually wins the spreadsheet. The snowball can win the human.

Choose deliberately, make minimum payments on time, and direct the freed cash flow toward assets once the debt is gone.
